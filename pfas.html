About PFAS

This page provides a brief overview of PFAS, common exposure pathways, potential health effects, and blood testing, with links to current guidance and information from the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR).

What are PFAS?

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a large group of man-made chemicals that have been used in industry and consumer products worldwide since the 1940s.

PFAS have been used in products such as water-repellent clothing, stain-resistant fabrics and carpets, some cosmetics, some firefighting foams, and products designed to resist grease, water, or oil.

PFAS can migrate into soil, water, and air when they are produced and used. Many PFAS do not break down readily, allowing them to persist in the environment and accumulate in people and animals over time.

Environmental Presence and Exposure

PFAS are widespread in the environment and have been found in water, soil, air, food, people, and animals. Exposure can occur through several pathways.

How can people be exposed to PFAS?

  • Drinking water from PFAS-contaminated municipal sources or private wells
  • Eating food produced near places where PFAS were used or made
  • Eating fish or wild game contaminated by PFAS
  • Accidentally swallowing or breathing contaminated soil or dust
  • Eating food packaged in materials that contain PFAS
  • Using some consumer products that contain PFAS
  • Working with PFAS or materials that contain PFAS

Exposure pathways vary among people and populations. Children and people whose work involves PFAS may experience different types or levels of exposure than the general population.

Potential Health Effects

According to ATSDR (CDC) , epidemiological evidence suggests associations between increased exposure to specific PFAS and certain health effects, including:

  • Increased cholesterol levels
  • Lower antibody response to some vaccines
  • Changes in liver enzymes
  • Pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia
  • Small decreases in birth weight
  • Kidney and testicular cancer

Research is ongoing. The risk of health effects associated with PFAS depends on factors such as the amount, frequency, route, and duration of exposure; individual characteristics; and other determinants of health.

PFAS Blood Testing

Nearly all people in the United States have measurable amounts of PFAS in their blood. PFAS blood tests can measure levels of certain PFAS, but they cannot identify a current health problem, predict future health outcomes, or determine treatment.

ATSDR notes that blood testing is most useful as part of a scientific investigation or health study. Individuals considering PFAS blood testing should discuss its limitations, risks, and potential benefits with a healthcare provider.
